How to read your financial statements

Financial statements are the reports that show how your business is doing. The main 3 are the profit and loss statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement. Valezar & Associates Inc., an accounting and tax firm in Miami, FL, prepares monthly financial statements for its bookkeeping clients and explains them in plain language.

The 3 statements

What does each statement tell you?

Each one answers a different question about your business.

Profit and loss statement

Did the business make money this month?

  • Revenue is what you earned from sales.
  • Cost of goods sold is the direct cost of what you sold.
  • Gross profit is revenue minus cost of goods sold.
  • Net profit is what remains after every expense.

Balance sheet

What does the business own and owe today?

  • Assets are what the business owns, such as cash and money customers owe you.
  • Liabilities are what the business owes, such as loans and unpaid bills.
  • Equity is what is left for the owners.
  • Assets always equal liabilities plus equity.

Cash flow statement

Where did the cash come from, and where did it go?

  • Operating cash flow comes from running the business.
  • Investing cash flow covers buying or selling equipment and property.
  • Financing cash flow covers loans, owner money in and owner draws out.
  • It explains why your bank balance changed.

Monthly review

Which questions should you ask every month?

5 questions that turn the reports into decisions.

  • Is gross profit steady as a share of sales, or is it slipping?
  • Which expenses grew faster than sales?
  • Are customers taking longer to pay you?
  • How many months of expenses does the business have in the bank?
  • Is the business on track to cover its tax bill this year?

Questions

Questions about financial statements

Why does my profit not match my bank balance?

Profit counts income and expenses when they are earned and owed. Your bank balance only moves when money comes in or goes out. Unpaid invoices, loan payments, owner draws and big purchases all make the two numbers differ.

How often should I look at my statements?

Every month. A monthly look lets you catch a problem while it is still small.

Which number should I watch first?

Start with cash in the bank compared with what you spend in a typical month. Then watch gross profit, because it shows whether your prices cover your direct costs.
